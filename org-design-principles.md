# Organizational Design Principles
These principles are working heuristics for a bylaws refactor: repeatable explanations for why a governance choice tends to be better for the organization. They are not an algorithm or a substitute for discernment. Principles can and should be held in tension, and applying them still requires judgment about the particular case. A useful warning sign is when a good principle is pushed so far that it becomes its own antipattern.

## Rules and judgment
### 1. Don't make rules for things that don't need rules
If nobody needs to require, prohibit, authorize, or constrain something, it usually does not need a governance rule. Ordinary freedom should remain ordinary.

### 2. Rules should be accountable and enforceable
A rule should do something: say what may, must, or must not happen, who has authority, and what limits that authority. Philosophy, aspirations, and values are not rules by themselves.

### 3. Use a clear rule when judgment isn't needed
Human judgment is unavoidable, but avoid ambiguity that serves no purpose. If something can be stated clearly and mechanically, don't make people repeatedly interpret it.

### 4. Governance is ultimately between people
Organizations are made of people and relationships. Trust, interpretation, disagreement, values, and negotiated meaning cannot be designed away. Hold this in tension with using clear rules: remove unnecessary judgment without pretending governance can become objective or mechanical.

### 5. Be precise at the seams; leave freedom inside
Define interfaces, handoffs, rights, obligations, thresholds, and authority boundaries clearly. Within a clear scope, leave people and member organizations room to act.

## Authority and organization of rules
### 6. Make authority explicit, scoped, and recorded
It should be possible to tell who has authority, where it comes from, what it covers, what limits it, and how it can change. This applies at every level of the organization, not just to Board delegations.

### 7. Don't be redundant
Define a rule once. Repeating it in multiple places creates drift, contradiction, and uncertainty about which version controls.

### 8. Don't copy higher-level rules; cite them
If state law, the Articles, or another higher authority already establishes something, don't re-legislate it below. When it helps the reader, summarize it briefly and link to the controlling source.

### 9. Keep related rules together
Rules governing the same authority, process, or handoff should live together when possible. Readers should not have to reconstruct one process from fragments scattered across the system.

## Minimal power and minimal machinery
### 10. Keep governance surface area low
Every rule, exception, role, threshold, and approval step creates another place for ambiguity, conflict, gaming, or failure. Prefer fewer, cleaner governance interfaces when they can do the same work.

### 11. Prefer systems that are simple to understand and run
Administrative and cognitive complexity are real costs. Prefer systems ordinary participants can understand and operate without specialized procedural expertise.

### 12. Follow subsidiarity
Put authority and responsibility at the lowest level capable of handling the work. An organization may need a center for genuinely shared functions, but the existence of a central body is not itself a reason to move decisions upward.

### 13. Use the least power necessary
Use the least powerful mechanism and the narrowest authority that can adequately do the job.

Technically: prefer simpler, less expressive tools and representations when they are sufficient. Plain documents and links are often easier to inspect, combine, preserve, and replace than executable or highly stateful systems.

Institutionally: give people and bodies no more authority than their responsibilities require.

## Resilience and reality
### 14. Design for antifragility and resilience
Governance should withstand mistakes, disagreement, missed deadlines, turnover, and stress without one failure threatening the whole system. Where possible, stress should reveal useful information and make recovery or improvement easier.

### 15. Don't make unrelated processes depend on each other
A missed event, delayed election, or incomplete administrative step should not disable unrelated authority or work unless that dependency is actually necessary.

### 16. Design for the organization you actually have
Governance should fit the organization's real capacity, participation, structure, and available people, not an imagined fully staffed nonprofit. Rules requiring capacity the organization does not have become dead text or failure points.

### 17. Don't mistake convention for necessity
Don't preserve a structure just because nonprofits commonly have one. It should be justified by law, the organization's actual needs, member needs, or real human constraints, not convention or inherited boilerplate.

### 18. Use modern tools; don't inherit obsolete constraints
Older governance systems were designed around limits we may no longer have. Use asynchronous communication, persistent digital records, collaborative documents, automation, and other modern tools when they remove real constraints.

Hold this in tension with least power: having access to more capable technology does not mean the more complicated tool is better.

### 19. Friction can be a useful signal; don't build machinery just to suppress it
Disagreement, delay, discomfort, or resistance may reveal unclear authority, weak capacity, conflicting expectations, or a deeper design problem. Before adding procedure to make the friction disappear, ask what is producing it.

Governance should help people deal with real conflicts and constraints, not merely hide the signals that something is wrong.
